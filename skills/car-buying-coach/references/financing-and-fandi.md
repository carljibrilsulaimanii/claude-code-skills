# Financing & the Finance Office (F&I)

The finance office — where you sign after agreeing on the car — is where
**60–70% of a dealership's profit** is made. Everything in this room is
negotiable, and almost everything is marked up. Slow down here.

## Framing: let them think they'll finance

Statistically a dealership makes far more on a financed deal (~$4,000 average)
than a cash or outside-pre-approval deal (~$1,000). That's leverage *for you* on
the price of the car. So even if the user plans to pay cash:

- **Don't announce "I'm paying cash" or "I'm already pre-approved."** Say you'd
  finance **if the rate is right.** This keeps the finance profit on the table as
  a bargaining chip and earns a better discount on the car.
- If they truly want to pay cash, "the right rate" can quietly be a number the
  dealer can't hit (say 0–1%). They don't need to know that. A cash buyer can
  also take a promotional low-APR loan to capture a finance-tied rebate, then pay
  it off after a payment or two — sometimes worth thousands, for a few dollars of
  interest. (Always check the rebate's terms for clawbacks first.)

## Getting the best interest rate

Tommy considers rate negotiation more valuable than price negotiation, because a
higher rate quietly costs thousands over the life of the loan.

1. **Check manufacturer incentivized rates first (new cars).** Go to the
   manufacturer's website and see what promotional APR the car qualifies for
   (e.g., 0.9%, 1.9%). These are set by the manufacturer, not the dealer.
2. **Get real pre-approvals (used, or if no promo rate).** Shop a few credit
   unions and banks — PenFed, Navy Federal (if eligible), Consumers Credit Union,
   and the user's local credit union are common strong options. Find the best
   rate you actually qualify for.
3. **The credit-inquiry myth is false.** Multiple auto-loan pulls within a short
   window (roughly 14–45 days depending on the scoring model) count as a *single*
   inquiry. Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion all confirm this. Shopping the rate
   does not meaningfully ding a score, so shop freely.
4. **Tommy's contrarian move: tell them your rate and ask them to beat it.** Many
   gurus say hide your rate and make them beat an unknown. Tommy — six years in
   finance — disagrees: if you've genuinely found a great rate, give the dealer a
   real target and ask them to beat it by **at least a half to a full percent.**
   A dealer with no target just pulls their best *advertised* rate; a dealer with
   a strong number to beat calls in favors with their banks. They won't always
   beat it, but a quarter- or half-point improvement is a real win, and if they
   can't beat it you simply use your pre-approval.
5. **The rate is negotiable even when they say it isn't.** The bank contract
   itself typically states the APR is negotiable. If a dealer beats your rate by
   any amount, financing with them is usually the easier path — just quadruple-
   check the contract for products you didn't agree to (see below).

## GAP insurance

GAP (Guaranteed Asset Protection) covers the "gap" between what you owe and what
insurance pays if the car is totaled while you're upside down on the loan. It's a
**genuinely good product for the right buyer** — and simultaneously one of the
most profitable, most marked-up items in the finance office.

- **Who needs it:** anyone financing with little down (under ~20%), a long loan
  (over ~5 years), a high interest rate, or negative equity rolled in. If you're
  leasing, it's usually already included (confirm). Paying cash or with lots
  down: usually skip.
- **Price:** dealers start high — $1,500–$2,000. A good GAP policy costs roughly
  **$350–$550.** Name your number ("$450, take it or I'll get it elsewhere") and
  hold; they often take it just for the "penetration" credit.
- **Quality varies — read the fine print for:** (a) does it pay off **actual cash
  value** or the higher **retail value**? (b) what **percentage** does it cover
  (100–150%; 150% is best)? (c) **deductible reimbursement**? (d) is it
  **cancelable/refundable** if you pay the loan off early? Best case: retail
  value, 150%, deductible covered, fully refundable.

## Extended warranties (service contracts)

- **Buy for peace of mind, not because you expect to use it** — like insurance
  against a rare, expensive failure. If you *expect* to need it, maybe rethink the
  car.
- **Skip it on Toyota, Lexus, and Mazda** — they're rock-solid to ~100k miles,
  and most warranties only cover to ~100k anyway, so the risk window is tiny.
  Brands where it's more often worth it: Audi, Land Rover, BMW, Chevy, Jeep, Ford,
  Ram, Lincoln — *if the price is right.*
- **Price:** they're marked up 2–3×. A fair price is roughly **$800–$2,200**;
  above that it's rarely worth it. Never pay the first number (people get quoted
  $3,000–$5,000). State what you'll pay ("I'll do $1,500") and stay firm; they
  drop toward their cost as you near signing.
- **Watch the mileage math.** A warranty that only covers to 100k miles doesn't
  protect the high-mileage years — so "I'll keep it to 200k, so I want the
  warranty" is backwards.
- **Buy it out of state / after the sale to shop it.** Some states (e.g., Florida)
  legally can't negotiate warranty price. You can buy a manufacturer warranty
  later from *any* same-brand dealership — call around after purchase and let
  them fight for the sale near cost.

## Before you sign anything

- **Read the two documents that matter, line by line:** the **vehicle purchase
  agreement** and the **finance/bank contract.** You can skim the pile of routine
  forms, but these two need every line checked — this is where surprise GAP,
  warranties, or add-ons appear that you never agreed to.
- **Confirm the out-the-door number matches** what you agreed to, with no fees
  that crept back.
- **Get a signed "we-owe" (a "wheel")** for anything they promised — a repair, a
  second key, a floor mat. Verbal promises evaporate; a signed we-owe is
  enforceable.
- **Learn to say no.** They'll be warm and helpful and then push products. You're
  allowed to decline every one. In the worst case, you can walk — the car will be
  there tomorrow, or another one will.
