# Dealer Tactics, Add-Ons & Red Flags

A field guide to what dealers do and how to answer it. When the user forwards you
something a salesperson said, find it here and give them the counter (ready-to-
send versions in `message-templates.md`).

## Add-ons: what you pay for and what you don't

On any out-the-door breakdown, only these line items are legitimate:

1. **Price of the vehicle**
2. **Sales tax**
3. **Doc fee** (dealer documentation fee — everyone charges one; it varies by
   state/dealer and is roughly fixed)
4. **Title & license / registration** — government fees (in some states this is
   several small line items)

**Everything else is an add-on you generally should not pay for**, including:
nitrogen-filled tires, paint/ceramic protection, fabric/interior protection,
VIN etching, LoJack/theft protection, "appearance protection," and bundled
"protection packages" or "convenience packages."

Why they're worthless:

- The dealer's paint/ceramic coating is a low grade that's barely distinguishable
  from a good wax — nothing like a real detailer's ceramic coat.
- Nitrogen in tires is negligible; sometimes they just swap on green valve caps.
- **These products are often not even applied.** Dealers routinely charge for
  paint protection or nitrogen and skip the actual work when they're busy — and
  there's no way for you to verify it was done.
- Any real benefit is usually a warranty attached to the product, and those
  warranties are mostly toothless (five-year paint-fade protection on a car whose
  clear coat won't fade for 10–15 years).

**The newest dealer trick:** price the car very low online to win the search,
then pile mandatory-looking add-ons onto every car to make the margin back. About
60–70% of dealers still do this. When a manager "generously removes" an add-on as
a favor, remember they never should have charged it.

### How to handle add-ons without being a jerk

Being combative loses deals. Instead:

1. **Hear them out first.** Ask what's in the package and what value it adds.
   Occasionally there's something you actually want — window tint in Texas or
   Florida, for example, can be worth keeping even at a small premium.
2. **Say which items (if any) you see value in, and decline the rest.** "I'll pay
   for the tint since I want it, but not the nitrogen, the maintenance plan, or
   the appearance package."
3. **Use competition.** "I have quotes from other dealers with none of these
   add-ons. I'm fine with the discount you're offering, but the add-ons need to
   come off — or I'll go with one of those." Then wait. Someone drops the fees.
4. **Then guard against the bait-and-switch:** confirm the *final* out-the-door
   number, so they don't drop the add-on and raise the car price to match. Get it
   in writing.

## The "come in / presence is power" family of stalls

Nearly every dealer stall is really "get you into the showroom." The answer is
always a version of: *"I already know the car I want, I'm buying in the next day
or two, I just need your best out-the-door price. Other dealers are giving me
numbers over the phone, and I'll reward the one who does."* Specific counters:

- **"When can you come in?"** → "I've decided on the car, I don't need to see it
  again. I just need your best out-the-door price and I'm buying in a day or two."
- **"Presence is power / you'll get a better deal in person."** → "I appreciate
  that, but I'm not comfortable coming in until I have all the numbers. Other
  dealers are giving them to me over the phone — if you can't, I understand, I'll
  work with them."
- **"We don't do numbers over the phone / it's corporate policy."** → "No problem
  — I work with dealers who will. Any dealership that hides numbers up front tends
  to be difficult in person too. Good luck." (And mean it — move on. This is a
  filter, not a wall.)
- **"This is my best price, my manager won't go lower."** → "Understood — I've
  got lower quotes right now, so I'll probably go with one of those. Thanks for
  your time." Result: either they ask to see the quote (they'll go lower) or they
  let you walk (it really was their floor). Often they call back the next day.
- **"This deal is only good today / only if you come in today."** → Usually a red
  flag; deals almost always hold within the same calendar month. Call the next
  day and treat it as still valid. If they balk, that's a dealer to avoid.
- **"These add-ons are mandatory."** → They're not; it's glorified markup. Nothing
  legally forces you to buy paint protection or nitrogen. Decline and use
  competition.

## The finance-office pressure

They'll be kind — water, snacks, the lounge — and then push products and payment
talk. Two defenses:

- **Learn to say no**, calmly and repeatedly. It's their job to get to "yes";
  it's your right to decline every add-on, warranty, and GAP pitch.
- **Never let the conversation move to monthly payment.** Payment is where they
  hide markup (rate, term, cash down, fees all get shuffled to hit a number).
  Keep everything on the out-the-door price and do the payment math yourself.

## Red flags worth walking away over

- A salesperson who berates you for not driving in, or manufactures fake urgency
  ("the car might not even be here").
- A quote that seems too good to be true — dig into the full breakdown; there's
  often an add-on buried in the fine print (a low headline price plus a surprise
  "convenience package").
- A car advertised that turns out to be "not available" or perpetually "in
  transit" — sometimes a bait listing to generate leads.
- A used car with a bank buyback on the Carfax, or that's sat 180+ days with no
  obvious reason.

## At delivery

- **Inspect the car in good light, washed, before signing** — even a brand-new
  car. Scratches, dents, or missing accessories are yours the moment you drive
  off. Refuse to sign until issues are fixed or put on a signed we-owe.
- **Confirm two keys.** Cars should come with two; replacement key fobs are
  expensive, and "I think it has two" often becomes one after you've signed. Make
  them show you both, or negotiate the second key in.
- **Get a signed we-owe** for anything promised but not yet done.
