# Choosing the Car & Knowing a Good Price

## Shopping phase vs. buying phase

The single biggest mistake buyers make is collapsing these two into one rushed
trip. Keep them separate.

**Shopping phase** is like walking around Target picking things up. You're
looking, sitting, test driving, imagining the car in your life. You are *not*
buying. In this phase, hold a hard line:

- **Test drive 3–5 cars**, ideally back-to-back in one day (dealerships tend to
  cluster on the same strip). Even if you've driven Hondas your whole life, drive
  the Mazda, the Toyota, the BMW if it's in budget. You're about to make one of
  the largest purchases of your life; spend the afternoon.
- **Never go to numbers.** Tell the salesperson plainly: "I'm in the shopping
  phase, I'm not talking numbers today, and I don't want any pressure — if I get
  pressure I'll just leave." A low-pressure dealership will respect that.
- **Never hand over your keys or driver's license.** They'll ask for the keys to
  "appraise your trade" — this is really to anchor you and start the process.
  Just say you're not trading today.
- Aim for the **right car, not the perfect car.** Perfectionism is a major driver
  of buyer's remorse. Go home, make a quick pros/cons list, then decide.

**Buying phase** is everything after you know the car. It happens at home, over
text and phone — never emotionally, never in the showroom. The rest of this skill
is the buying phase.

## What a good price actually is (new cars)

Ignore the jargon. Online guides and "gurus" throw around **invoice**, **holdback**,
and **triple net** (invoice minus holdback minus marketing money). You don't need
any of it, and chasing invoice can actively cost you money — dealers sell *below*
invoice all the time, because they make money on holdback, finance, and volume
bonuses, and they need to move new metal to earn more inventory.

The rule of thumb that replaces all of it:

> **A good deal on a new car is 7–10% off MSRP, before rebates, plus tax/title/license.**

- **7–10% off MSRP** is the discount on the car itself.
- **Before rebates** — rebates stack *on top* of that discount. Don't let a
  dealer count a rebate you already qualify for as if it were their discount.
- **Plus TTL** — tax, title, and license are government fees you pay no matter
  what. They are not negotiable and not the dealer's to waive.

Some cars break this rule because the **market**, not the dealer, sets the price.
A genuinely supply-constrained or in-demand car (a hot trim, a fresh redesign)
may only move a few hundred off MSRP, or even sit at MSRP. That's not bad
negotiating — it's the market. Conversely, a slow-selling car or one with big
manufacturer incentives can go well beyond 10% off. Competition (below and in
`getting-quotes.md`) is how you discover where the real market is for *this* car.

**Rebate vs. dealer discount** — always separate these two. A *dealer discount*
is money the dealership takes off out of its own pocket. A *rebate* is money you
qualify for from the manufacturer (and sometimes from your employer, military
service, college grad status, loyalty, etc. — always ask what you qualify for).
Confusing them is how a dealer makes a mediocre discount look generous.

## Used cars

The process is identical whether it's a $15k Corolla or a $140k sports car. What
changes is the homework:

- **Become an expert on the one car.** Pick a single vehicle and learn it cold —
  common flaws, known issues, maintenance costs, and how many are for sale in
  your radius. A spreadsheet of comparable listings tells you the real market
  price fast.
- **Don't buy the cheapest one, and don't buy the most expensive one.** The
  cheapest car in the market is cheapest for a reason. Find the best-quality car
  at a fair price, not the rock-bottom listing.
- **Get a pre-purchase inspection (PPI)** whenever there's any doubt. A service
  like Lemon Squad runs ~$200 and will even go to the dealership. On a five- or
  six-figure purchase, that's cheap peace of mind.
- **Read the Carfax like a detective.** Beyond accidents and service history,
  watch for a **lease buyback or bank buyback** — a bank buyback often means
  someone defaulted, which correlates with a car that wasn't cared for. Red flag.
- **Ignore the "days on lot" myth.** A car sitting 90+ days does *not* guarantee a
  huge discount. Dealers actively re-price against third-party sites (Cars.com,
  CarGurus, AutoTempest) and mark down on a schedule, so by 90 days there's often
  *less* markup left, not more. And if a desirable car has sat that long, ask
  *why* before assuming it's a bargain.

## Choosing which dealership

- **Volume vs. value dealerships.** Volume stores want to move as many cars as
  possible and will deal hard on price. Value stores sell the experience at a
  premium. For the best discount, aim at volume dealers.
- **Read recent reviews.** Skip the service-department reviews and read the last
  month of *sales* reviews. Look for patterns — surprise fees, bait-and-switch —
  before you engage.

## Judging a deal in one line

If the user has real competing quotes and nobody will beat their number by a
meaningful amount, they have a good deal. That's the whole test. Don't torch a
fair deal over $50 — reward the dealer who was straight with them.
